# Week 12 — Friday

Not a defence. A review, and a handover.

The design work ended in week 11. This hour is about whether they can carry it, and what
they do next.

---

## Before they arrive

Read `MOCKS.md` and `READINESS.md`. Watch two minutes of their week-8 recording and two
minutes of their tenth.

Check three things:

- **Are the scores honest?** Ten mocks that all score 4s are ten mocks that were not scored.
  Look for a mock scored 1 on `break`; its absence is the tell
- **Is the trend real, or is it noise?** `progress` over ten mocks with the same weakest
  pass throughout is a student who has been practising what they are already good at
- **Does `READINESS.md` name a specific weakness**, or is it "I need more practice"?

---

## Part 1 — One last mock (20 min)

Run it properly. A brief they have not seen, forty-five minutes compressed to twenty by
telling them at minute one: *"we have twenty minutes, so give me pass 1 and 2 quickly and I
want to spend most of it on decisions and failure."*

That compression is itself the test. A student who has internalised the budget adapts; one
who has memorised a script runs out of time.

Score it with them, out loud, using their own rubric.

---

## Part 2 — The comparison (10 min)

Play thirty seconds of the week-8 recording, then thirty seconds of the tenth.

Ask: *"What is different?"*

The answer is almost never knowledge. It is structure, arithmetic out loud, and reaching
failure modes before the clock. Let them say it — hearing themselves name the three things
that trained is worth more than being told.

---

## Part 3 — The handover (20 min)

Not scored. The most useful twenty minutes of the course.

1. *"Which pass is still weakest, and what would you do about it in one week?"*
2. *"Which design in your portfolio would you take into an interview, and why that one?"*
   — usually Project 2 or 3, and the reason should be about the decisions rather than the
   scale
3. *"What in this course do you now think was wrong, or overstated?"* — ask it seriously.
   Twelve weeks of being told to check sources should produce a student willing to check
   ours, and the good answers are specific
4. *"What will you do in the first month of a job that this course did not prepare you
   for?"* — the honest list is long: operations, existing systems, organisational
   constraints, and code
5. *"What is the first thing you will look up when somebody asks you to design something
   in a domain you do not know?"* — the answer should be a question, not a mechanism

---

## What to say at the end

Two things, and both plainly:

**What they can now do.** Take an ambiguous brief, size it, make three to five defensible
decisions, know how it fails, and say which of their claims are sourced. That is a real
skill and it took twelve weeks.

**What they cannot.** They have not operated any of this. They have not lived with a design
for two years and watched it be wrong. They have never had the argument where the right
design loses to the organisation. All of that is learned on the job, and pretending
otherwise would undo the honesty the course spent twelve weeks building.

The decision library is the thing to keep. It is short, it is theirs, and it will still be
useful in five years — which is more than most of what is in the course can claim.
